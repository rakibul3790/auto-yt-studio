--- Voice part 01 ---
[serious] March 1974.
A Japanese soldier walks out of the jungle in a tattered uniform.
His rifle still works.
And as far as he knows... World War Two is still going on.
[curious] That really happened.
And it's not the only story today that sounds made up.
[excited] An army that lost a war... to birds.
A town that couldn't stop dancing.
A wave of syrup taller than a house.
[chuckles] And a man who sold the Eiffel Tower.
[whispers] All true. Let's start with the birds.

--- Voice part 02 ---
[curious] In 1932, wheat farmers in Western Australia had a big, feathery problem.
About twenty thousand emus had marched out of the outback and into their fields, eating everything.
So the farmers asked the government for help.
[excited] And the government sent... the army.
A major named Meredith, two machine guns, and ten thousand rounds of ammunition.
[confident] On paper, this should have taken an afternoon.
[chuckles] It did not.
The moment the guns fired, the emus scattered in every direction.
The soldiers set an ambush at a watering hole, and a huge flock walked right toward them.
They got about a dozen birds. Then the gun jammed.
They even mounted a gun on a truck, but the ground was so rough that nobody could aim.
After about a week, the soldiers were called home.
Meredith later said that if he had a division with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds,
it could face any army in the world.

--- Voice part 03 ---
[curious] The farmers begged again, so the army went back for round two.
In the end, Meredith's report claimed nine hundred and eighty-six emus...
using nine thousand eight hundred and sixty bullets.
[laughs] Exactly ten bullets per bird.
Newspapers called it "the Emu War."
And the emus? [amused] Still there. Still eating the wheat.
The army had lost a war to birds.
[softly] Birds beating an army is funny.
What happened in one European city, four centuries earlier... was not.

--- Voice part 04 ---
[mysterious] July 14, 1518. Strasbourg.
A woman named Frau Troffea stepped into the street and started to dance.
No music. No reason.
She danced all day, until she collapsed.
[uneasy] The next day, on swollen, bleeding feet, she danced again.
By the end of the week, more than thirty people had joined her.
Within a month, some accounts say, four hundred.
People who wanted to stop... and couldn't.

--- Voice part 05 ---
[curious] The city's doctors blamed "hot blood," and their cure was simple: more dancing.
[sighs] So the city built a stage, hired musicians, and paid people to keep the dancers moving.
It made everything worse.
Some old records claim dancers collapsed and even died, though historians still argue about how many.
Finally, the city changed its mind.
Music was banned, and the dancers were taken to a shrine in the hills to pray.
Slowly, the dancing stopped.
[whispers] So what was it? Nobody knows for sure.
Many historians think it was mass panic, in a city worn down by failed harvests, record grain prices, and disease.
Fear spread from person to person, until it took over their bodies.
[softly] A whole town, dancing, and not one of them wanted to.
[tense] That town couldn't stop moving.
Four hundred years later, in Boston, something else started moving... and it weighed about thirteen thousand tons.

--- Voice part 06 ---
[calm] January 15, 1919. Boston's North End.
It was strangely warm for January, and the streets were full of people.
Near the harbor stood a giant steel tank.
Fifty feet tall. Ninety feet wide.
Filled with two point three million gallons of molasses.
[serious] And the neighbors had noticed something. The tank leaked.
Brown syrup oozed from its seams.
The company's fix? [scoffs] They painted the tank brown.

--- Voice part 07 ---
[tense] Just after noon, people heard a deep rumble... then a rattling, like machine guns.
That was the rivets bursting out of the steel.
[shocked] And then the whole tank split open.
A wave of molasses, twenty-five feet high, rolled through the streets at about thirty-five miles an hour.
Faster than any person can run.
It knocked buildings off their foundations, smashed into the steel supports of the elevated railway,
and swept up wagons, horses, and people.
[sad] Twenty-one people died. Around a hundred and fifty were hurt.
Rescuers struggled for days, because as the air cooled, the molasses grew thicker, trapping anyone caught inside.
[softly] Investigators found the tank had been badly built and barely tested.
After that, engineers had to sign off on projects like this.
[sighs] And locals said that for decades, on hot summer days, the neighborhood still smelled sweet.
[curious] That disaster came from cutting corners.
Our next one came from a very careful plan... to sell something that wasn't for sale.

--- Voice part 08 ---
[curious] In 1925, a smooth-talking con man named Victor Lustig read a small story in a Paris newspaper.
The Eiffel Tower was rusting, and repairs were costing the city a fortune.
[sly] Most people saw a news story. Lustig saw a product.
He had fake government letterhead made, posing as a top official from France's Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs.
Then he invited a handful of scrap metal dealers to one of the fanciest hotels in Paris.
There, he told them a secret.
[whispers] The city was going to tear down the Eiffel Tower and sell it for scrap.
And it all had to stay quiet.

--- Voice part 09 ---
[excited] One dealer, André Poisson, wanted the deal badly.
He was new in town and wanted to make a name for himself.
Then Lustig hinted that government officials don't earn very much...
So Poisson paid him a bribe, on top of the price.
[laughs] Lustig grabbed the money and caught a train to Vienna.
And Poisson? He was too embarrassed to go to the police.
[chuckles] Lustig watched the newspapers. Not a word.
So not long after, he came back... and tried to sell the tower again.
This time, a dealer did go to the police, and Lustig had to run.
Years later, he was caught in the United States, for printing fake money.
[amused] But for a few days in 1925, a scrap dealer truly believed he owned the most famous tower in the world.
[serious] Lustig lied, and everyone believed him.
Our last man was told the truth, again and again... and refused to believe it for twenty-nine years.

--- Voice part 10 ---
[serious] In December 1944, a young Japanese officer named Hiroo Onoda was sent to Lubang, a small island in the Philippines.
His orders: keep fighting, and never surrender.
As American forces landed, his commander, Major Yoshimi Taniguchi, made him a promise.
Whatever happens, we'll come back for you.
A few months later, World War Two ended. [softly] But no one came.
Leaflets fell from planes saying the war was over. Onoda decided they were fake.
Newspapers were left for him. Letters from his family. He believed none of it.
[sighs] So he stayed hidden in the jungle with a few other soldiers, still fighting his war.

--- Voice part 11 ---
[serious] And that war was real for the people of Lubang.
Over the years, Onoda's group killed local islanders. As many as thirty, by some accounts.
One by one, his companions were gone. By 1972, Onoda was alone.
[curious] Then, in 1974, a young Japanese traveler named Norio Suzuki arrived.
He'd told friends he was searching for three things: Lieutenant Onoda, a panda, and the Abominable Snowman, in that order.
[chuckles] He found the first one in four days.
The two became friends. But Onoda still wouldn't come out.
He would only stop if his commanding officer ordered him to.

--- Voice part 12 ---
[emotional] So Japan found Major Taniguchi, now an old man working in a bookshop, and flew him to the island.
On March 9, 1974, Taniguchi stood in front of Onoda and read him his final orders.
The war was over.
[softly] Onoda handed over his sword and his rifle. It still worked.
[sad] He had fought for twenty-nine years, for a war that had ended long before.
[emotional] And Taniguchi had promised to come back.
Twenty-nine years later... he did.
